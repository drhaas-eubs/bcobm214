F25 · SLIBRARY 7 — BUSINESS PLAN COMPONENTS

Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas

Alexander Osterwalder & Yves Pigneur (2010)

The Business Model Canvas (BMC) is a one-page strategic management template that describes a business model as nine interlocking blocks: Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partners and Cost Structure (Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010).

Nine building blocks of any business model on one page.

REFERENCE

Osterwalder, A. & Pigneur, Y. (2010) Business Model Generation. Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons.

F26 · SLIBRARY 7 — BUSINESS PLAN COMPONENTS

Maurya's Lean Canvas (applied)

Ash Maurya (2012)

The Lean Canvas (applied to business planning) is the same nine-block template introduced in Unit 2 (F11), here used as the architectural skeleton for Phase 1 of the BCOBM214 Business Plan, with each block treated as a hypothesis to be operationalised in subsequent plan sections (Maurya, 2012).

Startup-flavoured cousin of BMC.

REFERENCE

Maurya, A. (2012) Running Lean. 2nd ed. Sebastopol, CA: O'Reilly.

F27 · SLIBRARY 7 — BUSINESS PLAN COMPONENTS

Mintzberg's 5 Ps of Strategy

Henry Mintzberg (1987)

Mintzberg's 5 Ps of Strategy is a meta-definition of strategy that recognises five compatible meanings used in practice: Plan (intended action), Ploy (a manoeuvre to outwit competition), Pattern (consistency in a stream of decisions), Position (a place in the market relative to competitors), and Perspective (an organisational worldview) (Mintzberg, 1987).

Plan, Ploy, Pattern, Position, Perspective — five compatible definitions.

REFERENCE

Mintzberg, H. (1987) The Strategy Concept I: Five Ps for Strategy. California Management Review, 30(1), pp. 11–24.

F28 · SLIBRARY 7 — BUSINESS PLAN COMPONENTS

Hambrick & Fredrickson's Strategy Diamond

Donald Hambrick & James Fredrickson (2001)

The Strategy Diamond is a five-element framework holding that a complete strategy must answer five questions: Arenas (where will we be active?), Vehicles (how will we get there?), Differentiators (how will we win?), Staging (what will be our speed and sequence?), and Economic Logic (how will we obtain returns?) (Hambrick & Fredrickson, 2001).

Arenas · Vehicles · Differentiators · Staging · Economic Logic.

REFERENCE

Hambrick, D. C. & Fredrickson, J. W. (2001) Are You Sure You Have a Strategy? Academy of Management Executive, 15(4), pp. 48–59.

F29 · SLIBRARY 8 — GO-TO-MARKET & ROADMAP

McCarthy's 4Ps Marketing Mix

E. Jerome McCarthy (1960)

The 4Ps Marketing Mix is a tactical-marketing framework grouping the controllable variables a firm uses to influence customer demand into four categories — Product (what is offered), Price (what it costs), Place (where it is bought), and Promotion (how it is communicated) (McCarthy, 1960).

Product · Price · Place · Promotion.

REFERENCE

McCarthy, E. J. (1960) Basic Marketing: A Managerial Approach. Homewood, IL: Richard D. Irwin.

F30 · SLIBRARY 8 — GO-TO-MARKET & ROADMAP

Booms & Bitner's 7Ps Service Mix

Bernard Booms & Mary Bitner (1981)

The 7Ps Service Mix is an extension of McCarthy's 4Ps to services, adding three categories that capture the distinct nature of service businesses: People (those who deliver), Process (how the service unfolds), and Physical Evidence (the tangible cues of intangible service quality) (Booms & Bitner, 1981).

4Ps + People · Process · Physical Evidence.

REFERENCE

Booms, B. H. & Bitner, M. J. (1981) Marketing Strategies and Organization Structures for Service Firms. In Donnelly, J. H. & George, W. R. (eds) Marketing of Services. Chicago: AMA, pp. 47–51.

F31 · SLIBRARY 8 — GO-TO-MARKET & ROADMAP

BCG Growth-Share Matrix

Bruce Henderson, BCG (1970)

The Growth-Share Matrix is a portfolio-management tool that classifies a firm's businesses or products into a 2×2 grid defined by relative market share (high vs low) and market growth rate (high vs low), yielding four cells — Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks, and Dogs — each with distinct cash-flow and resource-allocation implications (Henderson, 1970).

Stars · Cash Cows · Question Marks · Dogs — portfolio thinking.

REFERENCE

Henderson, B. D. (1970) The Product Portfolio. Boston Consulting Group Perspectives.

F32 · SLIBRARY 8 — GO-TO-MARKET & ROADMAP

Moore's Bowling Alley

Geoffrey Moore (1995)

The Bowling Alley is a market-development sequencing model for high-tech products, in which a beachhead segment is dominated first ('the head pin'), and dominance is then leveraged into adjacent segments ('the next pin') via shared customer references, distribution channels, and 'whole product' completeness (Moore, 1995).

Win one beachhead, then knock down adjacent pins.

REFERENCE

Moore, G. A. (1995) Inside the Tornado. New York: HarperBusiness.
