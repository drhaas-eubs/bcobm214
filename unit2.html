F9 · SLIBRARY 3 — IDEA VIABILITY & PROOF OF CONCEPT

Ries' Lean Startup MVP Loop

Eric Ries (2011)

The Lean Startup MVP Loop is an iterative product-development cycle in which the smallest possible product (a Minimum Viable Product) is built, exposed to real customers, measured for actual behavioural response, and used to produce validated learning that informs the next cycle (Ries, 2011).

Build → Measure → Learn — minimise time from idea to validated learning.

REFERENCE

Ries, E. (2011) The Lean Startup. New York: Crown Business.

Blank's Customer Development

Steve Blank (2005)

Customer Development is a four-phase parallel process to product development — Customer Discovery, Customer Validation, Customer Creation, and Company Building — designed to ensure that the product being built is the one customers want and will pay for (Blank, 2005).

Discovery → Validation → Creation → Building. Get out of the building.

REFERENCE

Blank, S. (2005) The Four Steps to the Epiphany. Pescadero, CA: K&S Ranch.

F11 · SLIBRARY 3 — IDEA VIABILITY & PROOF OF CONCEPT

Maurya's Lean Canvas

Ash Maurya (2012)

The Lean Canvas is a one-page business model template adapted from the Business Model Canvas, with four blocks redesigned for pre-product-market-fit startups: Problem, Solution, Key Metrics, and Unfair Advantage replace Key Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, and Customer Relationships (Maurya, 2012).

One-page business model for early-stage startups.

REFERENCE

Maurya, A. (2012) Running Lean: Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works. 2nd ed. Sebastopol, CA: O'Reilly Media.

F12 · SLIBRARY 3 — IDEA VIABILITY & PROOF OF CONCEPT

Christensen's Jobs-to-be-Done

Clayton Christensen (2003, 2016)

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) is a theory of customer behaviour holding that customers do not buy products as such; they 'hire' products to make progress on a job that arises in their life — comprising functional, social and emotional dimensions (Christensen et al., 2016).

Customers don't buy products — they hire them to do a job.

REFERENCE

Christensen, C. M., Hall, T., Dillon, K. & Duncan, D. S. (2016) Competing Against Luck: The Story of Innovation and Customer Choice. New York: HarperBusiness.

F13 · SLIBRARY 4 — PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT & LIFECYCLE

Cooper's Stage-Gate Process

Robert G. Cooper (1986)

Stage-Gate® is a structured new-product development process alternating between work stages (Discovery, Scoping, Build Business Case, Development, Testing & Validation, Launch) and decision gates at which senior management chooses go / kill / hold / recycle for each project (Cooper, 1986).

Discovery → Scoping → Build → Test → Launch — go/no-go gates between each.

REFERENCE

Cooper, R. G. (1986) Winning at New Products. Reading, MA: Addison-Wesley.

F14 · SLIBRARY 4 — PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT & LIFECYCLE

Vernon's Product Lifecycle

Raymond Vernon (1966)

The Product Lifecycle (PLC) describes the four-phase trajectory most products follow over their commercial life — Introduction (slow growth, high cost, few competitors), Growth (rapid adoption, falling unit costs, entrants), Maturity (peak revenue, intense competition), and Decline (falling demand, exits) — with strategy and economics differing at each phase (Vernon, 1966).

Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline. Strategy shifts at each phase.

REFERENCE

Vernon, R. (1966) International Investment and International Trade in the Product Cycle. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 80(2), pp. 190–207.

F15 · SLIBRARY 4 — PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT & LIFECYCLE

Moore's Crossing the Chasm

Geoffrey Moore (1991)

Crossing the Chasm refers to the discontinuity in technology product adoption between visionary early adopters (who tolerate incomplete products) and pragmatist early-majority buyers (who demand whole-product solutions and references), and to the strategy of dominating a single beachhead segment to bridge that gap (Moore, 1991).

The gap between early adopters and early majority — and how to bridge it.

REFERENCE

Moore, G. A. (1991) Crossing the Chasm. New York: HarperBusiness.

F16 · SLIBRARY 4 — PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT & LIFECYCLE

Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations

Everett Rogers (1962)

Diffusion of Innovations is a sociological theory of how, why and at what rate new ideas and technologies spread through a social system, classifying adopters into five categories (Innovators 2.5%, Early Adopters 13.5%, Early Majority 34%, Late Majority 34%, Laggards 16%) and identifying five attributes that affect adoption rate (Rogers, 1962).

Innovators · Early Adopters · Early Majority · Late Majority · Laggards.

REFERENCE

Rogers, E. M. (1962) Diffusion of Innovations. New York: Free Press.
