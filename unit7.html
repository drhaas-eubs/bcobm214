F49 · SLIBRARY 13 — GROWTH LIFECYCLE

Greiner's Growth Curve

Larry Greiner (1972, revised 1998)

Greiner's Growth Curve is a developmental model describing the five sequential phases organisations pass through as they grow — Creativity, Direction, Delegation, Coordination, Collaboration — each ending in a predictable crisis (Leadership, Autonomy, Control, Red Tape, Growth itself) that forces structural transition to the next phase (Greiner, 1972, revised 1998).

Five phases (Creativity, Direction, Delegation, Coordination, Collaboration) and five crises.

REFERENCE

Greiner, L. E. (1972) Evolution and Revolution as Organizations Grow. Harvard Business Review, Jul–Aug, pp. 37–46.

F50 · SLIBRARY 13 — GROWTH LIFECYCLE

Adizes' Corporate Lifecycle

Ichak Adizes (1979 onward)

Adizes' Corporate Lifecycle is a developmental model treating organisations as living systems that pass through ten predictable phases — Courtship, Infancy, Go-Go, Adolescence, Prime, Stable, Aristocracy, Recrimination, Bureaucracy, Death — with characteristic pathologies and required leadership styles at each phase, and Prime as a state requiring constant cultivation rather than an endpoint (Adizes, 1979, 1988).

Courtship → Infancy → Go-Go → Adolescence → Prime → Aristocracy → Decline.

REFERENCE

Adizes, I. (1988) Corporate Lifecycles: How and Why Corporations Grow and Die and What to Do About It. Englewood Cliffs, NJ: Prentice Hall.

F51 · SLIBRARY 13 — GROWTH LIFECYCLE

Churchill & Lewis' 5 Stages of Small Business Growth

Neil Churchill & Virginia Lewis (1983)

Churchill & Lewis' 5 Stages of Small Business Growth is a developmental model identifying the five phases small businesses pass through — Existence (gaining customers), Survival (proving viability), Success (the disengage / grow choice), Take-off (rapid scaling), and Resource Maturity — with each stage characterised by distinct management problems, decision-making patterns, owner involvement and capital structure (Churchill & Lewis, 1983).

Existence · Survival · Success · Take-off · Resource Maturity.

REFERENCE

Churchill, N. C. & Lewis, V. L. (1983) The Five Stages of Small Business Growth. Harvard Business Review, May–Jun, pp. 30–50.

F52 · SLIBRARY 13 — GROWTH LIFECYCLE

Collins' Good to Great

Jim Collins (2001)

Good to Great is an empirically derived synthesis of seven concepts that distinguished sustained-outperformance companies from comparable peers — Level 5 Leadership, First Who Then What, Confront the Brutal Facts, Hedgehog Concept (intersection of passion × what-we-can-be-best-at × economic engine), Culture of Discipline, Technology Accelerators, and the Flywheel — derived from a five-year study of 11 companies that produced 15+ years of cumulative stock returns at least 3× the market (Collins, 2001).

Level 5 leadership · First Who Then What · Hedgehog Concept · Flywheel.

REFERENCE

Collins, J. C. (2001) Good to Great: Why Some Companies Make the Leap... and Others Don't. New York: HarperBusiness.

F53 · SLIBRARY 14 — GROWTH STRATEGIES

Ansoff's Growth Matrix

Igor Ansoff (1957)

Ansoff's Growth Matrix is a 2×2 strategic-planning framework crossing existing-vs-new markets with existing-vs-new products to yield four growth strategies — Market Penetration, Product Development, Market Development, and Diversification — with risk increasing clockwise from Market Penetration (lowest) to Diversification (highest) (Ansoff, 1957).

Markets × Products growth options — risk increases clockwise.

REFERENCE

Ansoff, H. I. (1957) Strategies for Diversification. Harvard Business Review, Sep–Oct, pp. 113–124.

F54 · SLIBRARY 14 — GROWTH STRATEGIES

Penrose's Theory of the Growth of the Firm

Edith Penrose (1959)

Penrose's Theory holds that firms are bundles of productive resources — particularly the services rendered by management — and that growth is bounded not by demand or capital but by managerial capacity. As current operations stabilise, managerial 'slack' becomes available for new ventures, creating an internal growth engine that distinguishes firms from markets (Penrose, 1959).

Firms grow when management capacity exceeds current operations.

REFERENCE

Penrose, E. (1959) The Theory of the Growth of the Firm. Oxford: Basil Blackwell.

F55 · SLIBRARY 14 — GROWTH STRATEGIES

McGrath's Discovery-Driven Growth

Rita McGrath (with Ian MacMillan, 1995, 2009)

Discovery-Driven Planning is a structured method for planning under uncertainty in which the desired financial outcome is specified first, the operating model required to achieve it is reverse-engineered, every key assumption is made explicit, and the plan becomes a sequence of experiments testing the riskiest assumptions first — converting hypotheses into facts before scaling commitment (McGrath & MacMillan, 1995, 2009).

Plan in reverse: define success, surface assumptions, test riskiest first.

REFERENCE

McGrath, R. G. & MacMillan, I. C. (1995) Discovery-Driven Planning. Harvard Business Review, Jul–Aug, pp. 44–54.

F56 · SLIBRARY 14 — GROWTH STRATEGIES

Hamel's Stretch & Leverage

Gary Hamel & C. K. Prahalad (1993)

Stretch & Leverage is a strategic resource-management framework prescribing two paired disciplines — setting Stretch ambition that deliberately exceeds current resources, and applying five Leverage modes (concentrating, accumulating, complementing, conserving, and recovering resources) to close the gap between ambition and resources — producing disproportionate growth from the same resource base (Hamel & Prahalad, 1993).

Build ambition that outruns resources; leverage what you have to close the gap.

REFERENCE

Hamel, G. & Prahalad, C. K. (1993) Strategy as Stretch and Leverage. Harvard Business Review, Mar–Apr, pp. 75–84.
