F41 · SLIBRARY 11 — INTRAPRENEURSHIP

Pinchot's 10 Commandments of Intrapreneurship

Gifford Pinchot III (1985)

Pinchot's 10 Commandments are a set of behavioural prescriptions for the corporate intrapreneur — the person seeking to create new ventures inside a large organisation — that name the political and cultural obstacles distinct from independent entrepreneurship and prescribe the practices that overcome them (Pinchot, 1985).

How to behave as an entrepreneur inside a corporation.

REFERENCE

Pinchot, G. III (1985) Intrapreneuring: Why You Don't Have to Leave the Corporation to Become an Entrepreneur. New York: Harper & Row.

F42 · SLIBRARY 11 — INTRAPRENEURSHIP

Govindarajan's 3-Box Solution

Vijay Govindarajan (2016)

The 3-Box Solution is a framework for sustaining corporate innovation that prescribes three concurrent activities — Box 1 (manage the present efficiently), Box 2 (selectively forget the practices and assumptions that no longer serve), and Box 3 (create the future through experimental ventures) — with deliberate balance across all three (Govindarajan, 2016).

Manage present · Selectively forget past · Create future — concurrently.

REFERENCE

Govindarajan, V. (2016) The Three-Box Solution: A Strategy for Leading Innovation. Boston: HBR Press.

F43 · SLIBRARY 11 — INTRAPRENEURSHIP

McKinsey's Three Horizons of Growth

Baghai, Coley & White, McKinsey (1999)

The Three Horizons of Growth is a portfolio framework that classifies a firm's growth initiatives into three categories — Horizon 1 (extending and defending the core business), Horizon 2 (building emerging businesses with proven models that are scaling rapidly), and Horizon 3 (creating viable options for future businesses) — and prescribes concurrent investment across all three (Baghai, Coley & White, 1999).

H1 core · H2 emerging · H3 future — pipeline management.

REFERENCE

Baghai, M., Coley, S. & White, D. (1999) The Alchemy of Growth. Cambridge, MA: Perseus Books.

F44 · SLIBRARY 11 — INTRAPRENEURSHIP

Innovation Ambition Matrix

Bansi Nagji & Geoff Tuff (2012)

The Innovation Ambition Matrix classifies innovation investments along two dimensions (where to play × how to win) into three ambition zones — Core (optimising existing offerings for existing customers), Adjacent (expanding into new markets or new offerings), and Transformational (creating new markets with new offerings) — with empirical research suggesting a typical 70/20/10 resource allocation across the three zones (Nagji & Tuff, 2012).

Core · Adjacent · Transformational — typical 70/20/10 allocation.

REFERENCE

Nagji, B. & Tuff, G. (2012) Managing Your Innovation Portfolio. Harvard Business Review, May, pp. 66–74.

F45 · SLIBRARY 12 — INNOVATION, BD & LEADERSHIP

Drucker — Innovation vs Invention

Peter Drucker

Drucker's Innovation vs Invention distinction holds that invention is the act of creating something novel, while innovation is the disciplined process of converting that novelty into customer-recognised, market-validated value — a distinction that explains why most inventions never become innovations and why innovation is a managerial discipline rather than a creative event (Drucker, 1985).

Invention creates novelty; innovation creates value.

REFERENCE

Drucker, P. F. (1985) Innovation and Entrepreneurship. New York: Harper & Row.

F46 · SLIBRARY 12 — INNOVATION, BD & LEADERSHIP

Sørensen's Market-Oriented BD

Hans Eibe Sørensen (2012)

Market-Oriented Business Development is the discipline of integrating customer-derived market insight into strategic and operational decisions across the firm, positioning Business Development as a boundary-spanning function distinct from marketing (which manages demand creation) and sales (which manages transactions) (Sørensen, 2012).

BD as the cross-functional bridge between market insight, strategy and operations.

REFERENCE

Sørensen, H. E. (2012) Business Development: A Market-Oriented Perspective. Chichester: John Wiley & Sons.

F47 · SLIBRARY 12 — INNOVATION, BD & LEADERSHIP

Yukl's Influence Tactics

Gary Yukl (1980s onward)

Yukl's Influence Tactics is a research-derived typology of eleven distinct proactive tactics that leaders, BD professionals and managers use to influence the behaviour of others — including rational persuasion, inspirational appeal, consultation, ingratiation, exchange, personal appeal, coalition, legitimating, pressure, collaboration, and apprising — with empirical evidence on which tactics produce commitment vs compliance vs resistance (Yukl, 2010).

Eleven proactive influence tactics ranked by effectiveness.

REFERENCE

Yukl, G. A. (2010) Leadership in Organizations. 7th ed. Upper Saddle River, NJ: Pearson.

F48 · SLIBRARY 12 — INNOVATION, BD & LEADERSHIP

Goleman's Emotional Intelligence

Daniel Goleman (1995, 2002)

Emotional Intelligence (EI) is a four-quadrant capability model identifying the personal and social competencies that distinguish effective leaders, BD professionals and managers — Self-Awareness (recognising one's own emotions), Self-Management (regulating them), Social Awareness (recognising others' emotions), and Relationship Management (influencing them) — with empirical evidence linking EI to professional and BD effectiveness (Goleman, 1995, 2002).

Self-awareness · Self-management · Social awareness · Relationship management.

REFERENCE

Goleman, D. (1995) Emotional Intelligence. New York: Bantam Books.
