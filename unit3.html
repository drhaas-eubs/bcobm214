F17 · SLIBRARY 5 — MARKET RESEARCH METHODS

Porter's Five Forces

Michael Porter (1979)

Porter's Five Forces is a structural analysis of industry profitability that examines five competitive forces — rivalry among existing firms, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, and bargaining power of suppliers — to determine the underlying attractiveness and strategic position available within an industry (Porter, 1979).

Industry structure: rivalry, buyers, suppliers, entrants, substitutes.

REFERENCE

Porter, M. E. (1979) How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy. Harvard Business Review, Mar–Apr, pp. 137–145.

F18 · SLIBRARY 5 — MARKET RESEARCH METHODS

PESTLE Analysis

Aguilar (1967), Anonymous evolution

PESTLE is a structured environmental scan that examines six categories of macro-environmental forces — Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal and Environmental — that originate outside the firm but materially shape industry conditions, opportunities and risks (Aguilar, 1967, expanded over subsequent decades).

Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental — the macro context.

REFERENCE

Aguilar, F. J. (1967) Scanning the Business Environment. New York: Macmillan.

F19 · SLIBRARY 5 — MARKET RESEARCH METHODS

SWOT Analysis

Albert Humphrey, SRI (1960s)

SWOT is a strategic-planning matrix that organises situational analysis into four quadrants — Strengths and Weaknesses (internal, present-state attributes) and Opportunities and Threats (external, future-state forces) — and uses cross-quadrant intersections (SO, ST, WO, WT) to generate strategic options (Humphrey, 1960s; documented retrospectively).

Strengths · Weaknesses · Opportunities · Threats — internal × external 2×2.

REFERENCE

Humphrey, A. S. (2005) SWOT Analysis for Management Consulting. SRI Alumni Newsletter, December.

F20 · SLIBRARY 5 — MARKET RESEARCH METHODS

The Mom Test

Rob Fitzpatrick (2013)

The Mom Test is a customer-interview methodology built on three rules — talk about their life, not your idea; ask specifics about the past, not generics about the future; talk less, listen more — designed to extract reliable behavioural data even from interviewees who would, like a parent, instinctively avoid disappointing the entrepreneur (Fitzpatrick, 2013).

Three rules for talking to customers without lying to yourself.

REFERENCE

Fitzpatrick, R. (2013) The Mom Test: How to Talk to Customers and Learn If Your Business Is a Good Idea When Everyone Is Lying to You. Founder Centric.

F21 · SLIBRARY 6 — OPPORTUNITY IDENTIFICATION

Osterwalder's Value Proposition Canvas

Alexander Osterwalder & Yves Pigneur (2014)

The Value Proposition Canvas is a two-side visual tool for designing and testing fit between customer needs and product offerings. The right side maps the customer profile (jobs to be done, pains, gains); the left side maps the value proposition (products and services, pain relievers, gain creators); fit is achieved when left matches right (Osterwalder et al., 2014).

Customer profile (jobs, pains, gains) ↔ Value map (products, pain relievers, gain creators).

REFERENCE

Osterwalder, A., Pigneur, Y., Bernarda, G. & Smith, A. (2014) Value Proposition Design. Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons.

F22 · SLIBRARY 6 — OPPORTUNITY IDENTIFICATION

Kim & Mauborgne's Four Actions Grid

W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne (2005)

The Four Actions Grid is a strategic-positioning tool used to construct a new value curve by asking, of an industry's competing factors: which to Eliminate entirely, which to Reduce well below industry standard, which to Raise well above standard, and which to Create that the industry has never offered (Kim & Mauborgne, 2005).

Eliminate · Reduce · Raise · Create — the engine of Blue Ocean Strategy.

REFERENCE

Kim, W. C. & Mauborgne, R. (2005) Blue Ocean Strategy. Boston: Harvard Business Review Press.

F23 · SLIBRARY 6 — OPPORTUNITY IDENTIFICATION

TAM-SAM-SOM Market Sizing

Investor practice (1990s onward)

TAM-SAM-SOM is a three-tier market-sizing convention used in venture investment: Total Addressable Market (the entire universe of potential demand), Serviceable Available Market (the portion reachable with the firm's current model and geography), and Serviceable Obtainable Market (the portion realistically winnable in 3–5 years given competition and resources) (industry practice).

Total · Serviceable · Obtainable Market — three nested rings.

REFERENCE

Bensoussan, B. E. & Fleisher, C. S. (2008) Analysis Without Paralysis. Upper Saddle River, NJ: FT Press.

F24 · SLIBRARY 6 — OPPORTUNITY IDENTIFICATION

Christensen's Job-to-be-Done Map

Christensen et al. (2007)

The JTBD Map is a process diagram of the steps a customer takes to make progress on a defined job, where each step is rated on customer importance and current satisfaction; under-served steps (high importance, low satisfaction) reveal opportunity, and over-served steps reveal vulnerability to disruption (Christensen et al., 2007).

Map the steps of the customer's job and find under-served stages.

REFERENCE

Christensen, C. M., Anthony, S. D., Berstell, G. & Nitterhouse, D. (2007) Finding the Right Job for Your Product. MIT Sloan Management Review, 48(3), pp. 38–47.
