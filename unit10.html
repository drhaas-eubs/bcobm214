F73 · SLIBRARY 19 — PLAN EVALUATION

Sahlman's Frame (applied)

William Sahlman (1997)

Sahlman's Frame (applied) is the use of the four-element business plan structure introduced as F33 — People, Opportunity, Context, Risk & Reward — as a final-review checklist before plan submission and pitch delivery, ensuring each element has been addressed credibly and that no critical investor question is left unanswered (Sahlman, 1997).

People · Opportunity · Context · Risk & Reward — final review checklist.

REFERENCE

Sahlman, W. A. (1997) How to Write a Great Business Plan. Harvard Business Review, Jul–Aug, pp. 98–108.

F74 · SLIBRARY 19 — PLAN EVALUATION

Investor's 5-Lens Diligence

Composite — VC industry practice

The 5-Lens Diligence is a composite checklist representing the five dimensions investors mentally evaluate every pitch against — Team (can they execute?), Market (is it large and growing?), Product (does it work and is it differentiated?), Traction (is there evidence of pull?), and Deal (do the terms work?) — failure on any one usually kills the round (composite of VC industry practice).

Team · Market · Product · Traction · Deal.

REFERENCE

Macmillan, I. C., Siegel, R. & Subbanarasimha, P. N. (1985) Criteria Used by Venture Capitalists to Evaluate New Venture Proposals. Journal of Business Venturing, 1(1), pp. 119–128.

F75 · SLIBRARY 19 — PLAN EVALUATION

Risk-Reward Matrix

Risk management practice (general)

The Risk-Reward Matrix is a 2×2 (or 5×5) grid plotting risks by Likelihood (probability of occurrence) and Impact (severity if occurred), used to prioritise which risks demand active mitigation, which can be accepted, and which require contingency planning — with each high-impact risk assigned an owner and a mitigation plan (general risk-management practice; ISO 31000).

Likelihood × Impact 2×2 — colour code each risk and pair with mitigation owner.

REFERENCE

ISO (2018) ISO 31000:2018 Risk Management — Guidelines. Geneva: International Organization for Standardization.

F76 · SLIBRARY 19 — PLAN EVALUATION

NPV / IRR Quick Guide

Irving Fisher (1907) onward

Net Present Value (NPV) is the sum of discounted future cash flows minus the initial investment, with positive NPV indicating value creation; Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the discount rate at which NPV equals zero, used for project comparison; together with payback period they form the standard toolkit for evaluating capital investments and venture economics (Fisher, 1907; Brealey, Myers & Allen, 2020).

Discount future cash; compare to investment.

REFERENCE

Fisher, I. (1907) The Rate of Interest. New York: Macmillan.

F77 · SLIBRARY 20 — ITERATION & CLOSING

Build-Measure-Learn (recap)

Eric Ries (2011)

Build-Measure-Learn (recap) is the application of Ries's Lean Startup loop (introduced as F9) to the capstone business plan: the plan is treated as a structured set of testable hypotheses with explicit experiments designed to validate the riskiest assumptions in the first 90 days post-submission, rather than as a forecast to be defended (Ries, 2011).

The capstone uses the plan as a hypothesis, not a promise.

REFERENCE

Ries, E. (2011) The Lean Startup. New York: Crown Business.

F78 · SLIBRARY 20 — ITERATION & CLOSING

Design Thinking 5-Stage

IDEO / Stanford d.school (2004 onward)

Design Thinking is a human-centred problem-solving methodology comprising five iterative stages — Empathise (deep customer research), Define (sharp problem framing), Ideate (divergent solution generation), Prototype (low-cost solution embodiment), and Test (validated learning with real users) — applied here to the business plan itself, ensuring the plan is built around real customer needs rather than founder assumptions (Stanford d.school, 2010s; Brown, 2008).

Empathise · Define · Ideate · Prototype · Test.

REFERENCE

Brown, T. (2008) Design Thinking. Harvard Business Review, Jun, pp. 84–92.

F79 · SLIBRARY 20 — ITERATION & CLOSING

Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle

David Kolb (1984)

Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle is a four-stage model of how learning happens — Concrete Experience (the lived event), Reflective Observation (thinking about the event), Abstract Conceptualisation (drawing portable lessons from the reflection), and Active Experimentation (testing the lessons in new situations) — with full learning requiring all four stages, and incomplete learning resulting from skipping any one (Kolb, 1984).

Concrete Experience → Reflective Observation → Abstract Conceptualisation → Active Experimentation.

REFERENCE

Kolb, D. A. (1984) Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development. Englewood Cliffs, NJ: Prentice Hall.

F80 · SLIBRARY 20 — ITERATION & CLOSING

Sarasvathy's Pilot-in-the-Plane

Saras Sarasvathy (2001)

Pilot-in-the-Plane is the integrating fifth principle of Effectuation, holding that the entrepreneur's role is to shape the future through action and stakeholder commitment rather than to predict and respond to it — keeping the entrepreneur at the controls of an evolving venture rather than at the mercy of forecasts (Sarasvathy, 2001).

The future is shaped, not forecast — entrepreneur stays at the controls.

REFERENCE

Sarasvathy, S. D. (2001) Causation and Effectuation: Toward a Theoretical Shift from Economic Inevitability to Entrepreneurial Contingency. Academy of Management Review, 26(2), pp. 243–263.
