F1 · SLIBRARY 1 — DEFINING ENTREPRENEURSHIP & BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT

Schumpeter's Creative Destruction

Joseph Schumpeter (1942)

Creative Destruction is the economic process by which entrepreneurs disrupt market equilibrium through new combinations — of products, methods, organisations, supply sources or markets — that destroy existing economic structures while simultaneously creating new ones of greater value (Schumpeter, 1942). It is the engine, not the disturbance, of capitalist economic change.

Entrepreneurs disrupt equilibrium through new combinations.

REFERENCE

Schumpeter, J. A. (1942) Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy. New York: Harper & Brothers.

F2 · SLIBRARY 1 — DEFINING ENTREPRENEURSHIP & BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT

Kirzner's Entrepreneurial Alertness

Israel Kirzner (1973)

Entrepreneurial Alertness is the cognitive readiness to notice, without deliberate search, profit opportunities that exist in disequilibrium markets — typically as price gaps, unmet demand or under-utilised resources (Kirzner, 1973). Alertness explains why some actors see opportunities others miss, and why profit is the reward for noticing first.

The entrepreneur's core function is alertness to overlooked opportunities.

REFERENCE

Kirzner, I. M. (1973) Competition and Entrepreneurship. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.

F3 · SLIBRARY 1 — DEFINING ENTREPRENEURSHIP & BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT

Drucker's 7 Sources of Innovation

Peter Drucker (1985)

The Seven Sources of Innovation Opportunity is Drucker's classification of where, in practice, innovations actually come from: the unexpected, incongruities, process needs, industry/market structure changes, demographics, changes in perception, and new knowledge (Drucker, 1985). Together they constitute innovation's discipline rather than its mythology.

Innovation is a discipline with seven systematic sources.

REFERENCE

Drucker, P. F. (1985) Innovation and Entrepreneurship. New York: Harper & Row.

F4 · SLIBRARY 1 — DEFINING ENTREPRENEURSHIP & BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT

Sarasvathy's Effectuation

Saras Sarasvathy (2001)

Effectuation is a logic of decision-making characterised by five interlocking principles — bird-in-hand, affordable loss, lemonade, crazy quilt, and pilot-in-the-plane — that expert entrepreneurs use under conditions of true (Knightian) uncertainty (Sarasvathy, 2001). Goals emerge from available means rather than means being chosen to achieve pre-given goals.

Five principles describing how expert entrepreneurs decide under uncertainty.

REFERENCE

Sarasvathy, S. D. (2001) Causation and Effectuation: Toward a Theoretical Shift from Economic Inevitability to Entrepreneurial Contingency. Academy of Management Review, 26(2), pp. 243–263.

F5 · SLIBRARY 2 — VISION, MISSION, PURPOSE & GOALS

Sinek's Golden Circle

Simon Sinek (2009)

The Golden Circle is a three-ring model of inspirational communication in which an organisation articulates its purpose ('Why'), distinctive process ('How'), and offerings ('What') in that order — from inside out — to engage the limbic brain where loyalty and decision-making reside (Sinek, 2009).

Start with Why → How → What. People buy why, not what.

REFERENCE

Sinek, S. (2009) Start With Why: How Great Leaders Inspire Everyone to Take Action. New York: Portfolio.

F6 · SLIBRARY 2 — VISION, MISSION, PURPOSE & GOALS

Collins & Porras' BHAG

Jim Collins & Jerry Porras (1994)

A Big Hairy Audacious Goal (BHAG) is a long-term, ambitious organisational goal — typically with a 10–30 year horizon — that is clear, compelling, tangible enough to know if it has been achieved, and audacious enough that achieving it transforms the organisation (Collins & Porras, 1994).

Big Hairy Audacious Goal — a 10–30 year clear, compelling, galvanising target.

REFERENCE

Collins, J. C. & Porras, J. I. (1994) Built to Last: Successful Habits of Visionary Companies. New York: HarperBusiness.

F8 · SLIBRARY 2 — VISION, MISSION, PURPOSE & GOALS

SMART Goals

George Doran (1981)

SMART is a five-attribute checklist used to test whether a stated goal is operationally usable: Specific (unambiguous), Measurable (binary or numeric), Achievable (within the actor's reach), Relevant (aligned to a higher-order objective) and Time-bound (with a deadline) (Doran, 1981).

Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — the operationalisation of vision.

REFERENCE

Doran, G. T. (1981) There's a S.M.A.R.T. Way to Write Management's Goals and Objectives. Management Review, 70(11), pp. 35–36.

Ries' Lean Startup MVP Loop

Eric Ries (2011)

The Lean Startup MVP Loop is an iterative product-development cycle in which the smallest possible product (a Minimum Viable Product) is built, exposed to real customers, measured for actual behavioural response, and used to produce validated learning that informs the next cycle (Ries, 2011).

Build → Measure → Learn — minimise time from idea to validated learning.

Christensen's Jobs-to-be-Done

Clayton Christensen (2003, 2016)

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) is a theory of customer behaviour holding that customers do not buy products as such; they 'hire' products to make progress on a job that arises in their life — comprising functional, social and emotional dimensions (Christensen et al., 2016).

Customers don't buy products — they hire them to do a job.

F13 · SLIBRARY 4 — PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT & LIFECYCLE

Cooper's Stage-Gate Process

Robert G. Cooper (1986)

Stage-Gate® is a structured new-product development process alternating between work stages (Discovery, Scoping, Build Business Case, Development, Testing & Validation, Launch) and decision gates at which senior management chooses go / kill / hold / recycle for each project (Cooper, 1986).

Discovery → Scoping → Build → Test → Launch — go/no-go gates between each.

REFERENCE

Cooper, R. G. (1986) Winning at New Products. Reading, MA: Addison-Wesley.

F14 · SLIBRARY 4 — PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT & LIFECYCLE

Vernon's Product Lifecycle

Raymond Vernon (1966)

The Product Lifecycle (PLC) describes the four-phase trajectory most products follow over their commercial life — Introduction (slow growth, high cost, few competitors), Growth (rapid adoption, falling unit costs, entrants), Maturity (peak revenue, intense competition), and Decline (falling demand, exits) — with strategy and economics differing at each phase (Vernon, 1966).

Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline. Strategy shifts at each phase.

REFERENCE

Vernon, R. (1966) International Investment and International Trade in the Product Cycle. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 80(2), pp. 190–207.

F15 · SLIBRARY 4 — PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT & LIFECYCLE

Moore's Crossing the Chasm

Geoffrey Moore (1991)

Crossing the Chasm refers to the discontinuity in technology product adoption between visionary early adopters (who tolerate incomplete products) and pragmatist early-majority buyers (who demand whole-product solutions and references), and to the strategy of dominating a single beachhead segment to bridge that gap (Moore, 1991).

The gap between early adopters and early majority — and how to bridge it.

REFERENCE

Moore, G. A. (1991) Crossing the Chasm. New York: HarperBusiness.

F16 · SLIBRARY 4 — PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT & LIFECYCLE

Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations

Everett Rogers (1962)

Diffusion of Innovations is a sociological theory of how, why and at what rate new ideas and technologies spread through a social system, classifying adopters into five categories (Innovators 2.5%, Early Adopters 13.5%, Early Majority 34%, Late Majority 34%, Laggards 16%) and identifying five attributes that affect adoption rate (Rogers, 1962).

Innovators · Early Adopters · Early Majority · Late Majority · Laggards.

REFERENCE

Rogers, E. M. (1962) Diffusion of Innovations. New York: Free Press.

F17 · SLIBRARY 5 — MARKET RESEARCH METHODS

Porter's Five Forces

Michael Porter (1979)

Porter's Five Forces is a structural analysis of industry profitability that examines five competitive forces — rivalry among existing firms, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, and bargaining power of suppliers — to determine the underlying attractiveness and strategic position available within an industry (Porter, 1979).

Industry structure: rivalry, buyers, suppliers, entrants, substitutes.

REFERENCE

Porter, M. E. (1979) How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy. Harvard Business Review, Mar–Apr, pp. 137–145.

Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental — the macro context.

F19 · SLIBRARY 5 — MARKET RESEARCH METHODS

SWOT Analysis

Albert Humphrey, SRI (1960s)

SWOT is a strategic-planning matrix that organises situational analysis into four quadrants — Strengths and Weaknesses (internal, present-state attributes) and Opportunities and Threats (external, future-state forces) — and uses cross-quadrant intersections (SO, ST, WO, WT) to generate strategic options (Humphrey, 1960s; documented retrospectively).

Strengths · Weaknesses · Opportunities · Threats — internal × external 2×2.

REFERENCE

Humphrey, A. S. (2005) SWOT Analysis for Management Consulting. SRI Alumni Newsletter, December.

F20 · SLIBRARY 5 — MARKET RESEARCH METHODS

The Mom Test

Rob Fitzpatrick (2013)

The Mom Test is a customer-interview methodology built on three rules — talk about their life, not your idea; ask specifics about the past, not generics about the future; talk less, listen more — designed to extract reliable behavioural data even from interviewees who would, like a parent, instinctively avoid disappointing the entrepreneur (Fitzpatrick, 2013).

Three rules for talking to customers without lying to yourself.

REFERENCE

Fitzpatrick, R. (2013) The Mom Test: How to Talk to Customers and Learn If Your Business Is a Good Idea When Everyone Is Lying to You. Founder Centric.

F21 · SLIBRARY 6 — OPPORTUNITY IDENTIFICATION

Osterwalder's Value Proposition Canvas

Alexander Osterwalder & Yves Pigneur (2014)

The Value Proposition Canvas is a two-side visual tool for designing and testing fit between customer needs and product offerings. The right side maps the customer profile (jobs to be done, pains, gains); the left side maps the value proposition (products and services, pain relievers, gain creators); fit is achieved when left matches right (Osterwalder et al., 2014).

Customer profile (jobs, pains, gains) ↔ Value map (products, pain relievers, gain creators).

Investor practice (1990s onward)

TAM-SAM-SOM is a three-tier market-sizing convention used in venture investment: Total Addressable Market (the entire universe of potential demand), Serviceable Available Market (the portion reachable with the firm's current model and geography), and Serviceable Obtainable Market (the portion realistically winnable in 3–5 years given competition and resources) (industry practice).

Total · Serviceable · Obtainable Market — three nested rings.

Christensen's Job-to-be-Done Map

Christensen et al. (2007)

The JTBD Map is a process diagram of the steps a customer takes to make progress on a defined job, where each step is rated on customer importance and current satisfaction; under-served steps (high importance, low satisfaction) reveal opportunity, and over-served steps reveal vulnerability to disruption (Christensen et al., 2007).

Win one beachhead, then knock down adjacent pins.

F37 · SLIBRARY 10 — PITFALLS & PLAN QUALITY

Eisenmann's 6 Patterns of Startup Failure

Tom Eisenmann (2021)

Eisenmann's Six Patterns is an empirical typology of recurring startup failure modes — Bad Bedfellows (founder-investor misalignment), False Start (skipping customer discovery), False Promise (misreading early traction), Help Wanted (failure to build the team), Cascading Miracles (multi-step plans that require everything to go right), and Speed Trap (over-investment in growth before unit economics work) — derived from a study of hundreds of failed ventures (Eisenmann, 2021).

Bad Bedfellows · False Start · False Promise · Help Wanted · Cascading Miracles · Speed Trap.

REFERENCE

Eisenmann, T. R. (2021) Why Startups Fail. New York: Currency.

F38 · SLIBRARY 10 — PITFALLS & PLAN QUALITY

Bhide's Hustle as Strategy

Amar Bhide (1986)

Hustle as Strategy is the contention that in industries with low entry barriers and high uncertainty — typical of most early-stage ventures — sustainable competitive advantage rarely comes from strategic positioning.
